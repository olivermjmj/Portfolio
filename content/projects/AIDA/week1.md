## History
A Large Language Model, or LLM, is essentially a very large mathematical model that takes your input and tries to predict what should come next.

When you ask an LLM a question, it does not think about the question in the same way a human would. Instead, it splits the text into smaller pieces called tokens and calculates what tokens are most likely to follow based on patterns it learned during training.

The history behind modern LLMs goes quite far back.

In 1955–1956, a group of researchers including John McCarthy worked on defining what would become the field of Artificial Intelligence, with the term "Artificial Intelligence" being introduced around the Dartmouth Summer Research Project.

AI has gone through several periods of hype and disappointment since then. Different forms of language processing and machine learning were used in systems such as virtual assistants, search engines and translation software, but they were still very different from the LLMs we use today.

One of the biggest turning points happened in 2017 when researchers at Google published the paper **"Attention Is All You Need"**.

The paper introduced the **Transformer architecture**.

The important part about Transformers is their use of an attention mechanism. Instead of processing text only as a sequence where every word depends on the previous one, the model can look at different parts of the input and determine which parts are relevant to each other.

This made it much easier to train very large language models on enormous amounts of data.

Modern LLMs such as GPT, Claude and Gemini are all built around ideas that came from this architecture.

## Real Life Applications
This technology is still relatively new, which means that we probably have not discovered all of its useful applications yet.

Some of the areas that will benefit, will be: *General learning and medicine**.

For learning, you essentially have access to a private tutor 24/7.

You can use an LLM as a simple sparring partner where you ask questions and discuss a subject, or you can develop larger applications that use an LLM to create a more personalized learning experience.

Instead of simply asking:

> "What is JPA?"

You could ask the model to explain JPA, give you an exercise, let you attempt it yourself and then review your answer afterwards.

This is where I think LLMs become much more interesting than simply using them as an advanced Google search.

The same applies to software development.

An LLM can help explain code, find mistakes, generate tests, brainstorm solutions and help with technologies you have not worked with before.

It can either help with small processes or become an actual part of a larger application through an API.

Medicine is another area with a lot of potential.

Doctors work with enormous amounts of information, and AI systems could potentially help with things such as analyzing medical information, finding patterns and assisting doctors when evaluating possible problems.

The important distinction is that the AI should be used as another tool available to the doctor, rather than blindly replacing the doctor's judgement.

## The Downsides
The biggest problem I see with this technology is that **human restraint will become harder than ever before**.

Just like social media and other ways of getting easy dopamine, you now have another extremely easy shortcut available at basically all times.

This is especially dangerous when learning.

An LLM can be one of the best learning tools available, but it can just as easily damage the learning experience if you use it incorrectly.

You are always one prompt away from simply getting the answer.

The bigger problem is that you can sometimes get the answer without even intending to.

You might ask:

> "Can you give me a hint?"

And instead of giving you a hint, the LLM basically solves the entire problem.

That means learning how **not** to use an LLM is probably going to become just as important as learning how to use one.

Another problem is reliability.

LLMs can sound extremely confident while being completely wrong. They are built to generate a likely response, not necessarily a factually correct response.

Because of that, important information still needs to be verified instead of blindly trusting whatever the model outputs.

There is also the economic side of LLMs.

Training and running these models requires a huge amount of computing power. Most commercial models therefore charge based on the amount of tokens being processed.

The cost of using LLMs could change significantly as the technology develops. Hardware becomes better and models become more efficient, but at the same time newer models require increasingly large amounts of infrastructure and computation.

This becomes especially important when developing applications around an LLM, because every user interaction can potentially have a direct cost.

There are also much larger ethical and security issues surrounding AI, such as misuse by governments, cybercrime, biological research, misinformation and other difficult subjects.

Those subjects are outside the scope of this week's introduction, but they are important when discussing where this technology could eventually lead.

## Conclusion
In the future, I think using LLMs will become similar to how search engines are used today. Everyone will be able to use them, but the quality of how they are used will vary greatly.

For example, my grandparents are able to use Google and YouTube, where they can get most common questions answered by searching for them. However, being able to judge the quality of the pages they find and determine whether something is actually a good source is outside their scope.

I think the same will happen with LLMs. Most people will be able to use them and get useful answers, but being able to judge the quality of those answers, verify the information, and know when the model is wrong will be a completely different skill.
